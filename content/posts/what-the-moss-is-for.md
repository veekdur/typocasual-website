Concrete was an argument about permanence. It is a material that says *this will still be
here*, and for about seventy years it is usually right. Then a gutter joint fails, and the
argument is over.

The interesting part is not the decay. It is that the decay is not random. Water finds the
same joints, every time. Moss arrives where the water sits, not where the design intended
anything to sit. The building is being edited by something that has never read the drawings.

## What actually arrives first

Not moss. Algae, then a film of it that holds moisture against the surface. Moss needs that
film the way a fire needs kindling. By the time you can see green, the work has been underway
for two winters.

1. A joint fails, or was never sealed properly.
2. Algae colonises the damp line.
3. Organic matter accumulates in the algae mat.
4. Moss establishes in the accumulated matter, not on the concrete.

This is why the growth on a building looks like it is following the water rather than the
sun. It is.

> The concrete did not fail. It was simply outlasted by something with a shorter memory and a
> faster schedule.

## Why it matters to a photograph

A photograph of a building being reclaimed is a photograph of two timescales at once. The
concrete is measured in decades; the growth is measured in weeks. Neither will wait for you
to set up the tripod.

The frames on the [sheet](/) are printed at one size because of this. Ranking them would
imply a judgement about the building, and the building is not making a judgement. It is just
losing, slowly, to something smaller.
