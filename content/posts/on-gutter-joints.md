Every reclaimed building I have photographed has the same origin story, and it is never
dramatic. Nobody pours a river through a wall. A joint between two precast panels opens by
two millimetres over a decade, and that is enough.

The gutter joint is the best place to see it because the gutter is where the building already
concedes that water will arrive. It has a system for water. The system is a channel and a
slope and a hope.

## The two-millimetre problem

At two millimetres, surface tension still holds water in the channel. At three, it starts to
creep. At five, it is a wick, and it is wicking into the one place in the assembly that was
never designed to be wet.

- The sealant fails first, because it is the softest thing there.
- The concrete around it does not fail; it is simply wet from then on.
- Wet concrete is a habitat. Dry concrete is not.

That is the whole mechanism. It fits in a sentence and takes four winters to finish.

## Photographing it honestly

The temptation is to shoot the drama — the big tree on the roof, the wall that has gone
entirely green. The more accurate frame is the small one. A joint, at close range, with a
single line of green running out of it.

That is the frame that tells you what is happening everywhere else on the building.
